\section{Data Collection}
\label{chap:data_collection}
In this chapter, we discuss the methods used to collect data during the project’s testing phase.

\subsection{Data Categories}
In Chapter \ref{chap:research_goal_and_questions} we briefly discussed our research goal and questions. These questions study player engagement, motivation, enjoyment, and habits. Consequently, we chose several methods for collecting data to answer these questions. The data we gather all speaks to the game’s social benefit because a player’s engagement, motivation, enjoyment, and habits affect the individual and affect the player’s surroundings.

\textbf{Player engagement}\\
Data about the player’s engagement was gathered to see how players engage in the game during the two weeks. For this data, it was interesting to see how game metrics and other players’ presence affect the player’s engagement in the app. This data coincides with RQ2.

\textbf{RQ2:} \textit{How do game design elements and social interaction affect the players’ engagement in our game concept?}

\textbf{Player motivation}\\
To collect data on the player’s motivation, we choose to investigate how the game metrics and presence of other players affect the player’s motivation and willingness to be active and do activities outside their home. The data collected on this matter lay the foundation for reasoning about RQ3.

\textbf{RQ3:} \textit{How do game design elements and social interaction affect the players’ motivation in our game concept?} \\

\textbf{Player enjoyment}\\
Data on player’s enjoyment was collected to investigate what makes the game enjoyable. Theory on what makes games enjoyable has been applied to the concept; hence, it was interesting to see how the game metrics affected the player’s enjoyment. Additionally, it was interesting to see how the game’s social aspects affect the player’s enjoyment. Data on player enjoyment will be used to reason about RQ4. 

\textbf{RQ4:} \textit{How do game design elements and social interaction affect the players’ enjoyment in our game concept?}

\textbf{Player habits}\\
As the game aims to be socially beneficial, it is interesting to investigate how it affects the player’s habits of being active outside their home. Data on this matter relates to RQ5.

\textbf{RQ5:} \textit{How does the use of our game concept affect the players’ habits?}

\subsection{Questionnaire}
\label{sec:data_collection_questionaire}
To collect quantitative data, the participants were asked to answer a questionnaire. The questionnaire included a set of questions and statements that lay the foundation to reason about our research questions. The participants were given the questions in Norwegian (see complete questionnaire in Appendix \ref{appendix:questionnaire}). The questionnaire comprised 33 statements and 25 questions grouped into five sections depending on what type of data they could reason about.

A five-point Likert scale was chosen for the statements, where the respondents had the alternatives: strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, and strongly agree. This type of ordinal data can be tested using the Mann-Whitney test. The nonparametric test is used to compare differences between two independent groups, with a null hypothesis that the distributions in the two sample groups are equal \cite{lamorte_mann_2017}.

\subsection{Interviews}
To collect qualitative data on the user’s perception, we invited some of the subjects to participate in individual interviews and to participate in group interviews. Our interviews were semi-structure, and the purpose was to discover new information and triangulate other data sources. The combination of individual and group interviews has several advantages. Group interviews are suitable for generating “more responses, and more varied responses, as one participant’s views are challenged by others or stimulate others to new ideas” \citep{oates_researching_2006}. On the other hand, single interviews are reliable as it lowers the barrier for voicing opinions that deviate from the masses. An essential consideration for group interviews is to make sure that the group participants are of similar status so that they feel comfortable with speaking their minds and sharing their opinions and thoughts with the group. This was taken into account when deciding what subjects to invite to a group interview.

The semi-structured interviews, individual and group, were focused on the topics related to our research questions (see interview guide in Appendix \ref{appendix:interview_guide}). 

To collect data from these interviews, we chose to take quick field notes and record audio using \href{https://www.microsoft.com/en-ww/microsoft-teams/group-chat-software}{Microsoft Teams}. This method allowed us to structure the interviews as a natural conversation rather than a formal interview setting.

\subsection{In-game Observations}
The observations of the user testing were not systematic observation, nor was it a typical \textit{participant observation}.  We found that the best way to observe the subjects was to participate during the testing passively. This presence allowed us to observe how the test subject played and interacted. The test subjects were informed beforehand of our passive participation, thus the observations could be categorized as overt. We chose the role of passive observers to not compromise the results. Our goal was to refrain from creating challenges and reacting to activities so that the players would not feel pressured to play the game.

\subsection{Application Usage Analysis}
To collect data on application usage, we added Google Analytics for Firebase to the game. This SDK captures several key events in the app that gives insight into user behavior. The toolkit also allows the configuration of custom events. These custom events let us capture events triggered by the players’ application usage. Examples of such events are; tracking what screens the players visit, how they interact, and how they respond to push notifications. The events also give insight into how players handle the “tracking” and “create new challenge” processes. This insight can indicate where in the process a player’s attention is lost.

Additionally, some of the data can be directly gathered from the database. Examples include the number of activities the users choose to publish, the number of challenges they have participated in, and the number of likes given. These numbers can be used to support or invalidate the participants’ engagement in the testing period.

\subsection{Data Collection Ethics}
Due to the need for user testing, we focused, throughout the project, on issues that could occur related to privacy. Therefore, it was important to be transparent with the participants with how we collect and present their data in the thesis.

It was a priority to develop the app to store as little as possible about the use. However, for the purpose of the study and the game concept, some information such as locations, activity, and user data was necessary.

As the project is dependent on user testing we started a dialogue with the Norwegian Centre for Research Data (NSD) to ensure that our research was completed in a secure, rightful, and ethical manner. This early adoption of communication resolved potential GDPR and privacy issues early on in the project’s phase.

Before launching the game on App Store and Google Play, we saw the need for creating a privacy policy (see Appendix \ref{appendix:privacy_policy}) required by said platforms. The privacy policy discloses how, why, and what data we collect, and we made it available on our website and in the settings menu inside the application.
