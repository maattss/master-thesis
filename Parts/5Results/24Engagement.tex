\section{Player Engagement}
\label{chap:player_engagement}
This chapter presents the data from the survey relevant to the player’s engagement in Hover. The results seek to answer \textit{RQ2: “How do game design elements and social interaction affect the players’ engagement in our game concept?”}. Described data will be used in Chapter \ref{chap:discussion} to discuss and evaluate the result of this research question.

Player engagement results are best expressed in numbers and figures, describing how and what mechanisms engage the players. Thus, the chapter is primarily based on quantitative data from the questionnaire and usage data from the application gathered from the database and Google Analytics.

\subsection{Questionnaire}
Table \ref{tab:statements_engagemet_answers} shows responses from the questionnaire’s section on engagement, where the participants were asked to take a stand on six statements. Though the statements in the questionnaire were originally a 5-point Likert scale (strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, and strongly agree), we have synthesized these into a 3-point Likert scale for readability (disagree, neutral, and agree). The questionnaire included statements about rewards, curiosity,  challenge, and activity level to investigate the player’s engagement.

 Additionally, average answers from both research groups, the student society (Avg S) and the classmates (Avg C), are presented. The average is calculated using responses weighted according to the original 5-point Likert scale, where strongly disagree equals 1 and strongly agreed is 5.

The responses to the statements were broadly distributed with both positive and negative results. Answers from S2 revealed that 52.7\% agreed that they continually got new achievements and rewards during the test period. New achievements increase the incentive for playing and thus are positive for player engagement. Challenges between friends also seemed to have a positive impact on the engagement with 57.9\% (S4) answering that it made them use the application more. In addition to this, almost half of the respondents answered in S6 that notifications made them use the application more. 

On the other hand, answers from S1, S3, and S5 were more on the negative side of the spectrum. 47.4\% (S1) disagreed that they were curious about which achievements they could earn in the game. In S3, 57.9\% of the participants answered that the game was not challenging enough. At last, answers from S5 reported that many of the users did not use reactions to a large degree.

The averages for the two groups revealed that the classmates generally agreed to the statements more than the student society group. All statements except S5 had a higher average for the group of classmates.

The participants were also asked to answer how many days during the test period they used Hover (Q10). The responses from the two groups are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:engagement_perceived_usage}. Most of the respondents stated that they used Hover \textit{a couple of days each week}.

In the questionnaire, respondents were given an option to leave additional comments regarding their perceived engagement. One participant wrote that they appreciated the push notification that reminded them to hover when they entered a Hover zone.

\textit{“It’s very nice that you get push alerts when you are in a zone where you can use Hover.”\footnotemark}

Another participant was not aware of this feature, likely because they did not provide appropriate permissions to the application. However, the player states that such a feature would have made them play the game more. 

\textit{“I think I would have used the app more if I had been notified when I was within a zone.”\footnotemark[\value{footnote}]}

\subsection{Application Usage Data}
Data from Firebase Analytics and the database reveals how the players have engaged in the game during the two weeks of testing. The usage data also presents the most used features in the application. 

Figure \ref{fig:engagement_daily_users} shows the number of daily users. Meaning the number of users that have opened the application at least once during that day. The data shows a clear tendency that the number of active users was decreasing during the testing period. Day 1 being the peek, and day 13 the lowest point. Both Mondays, days 1 and 8, are the peeks of their respective weeks. Another tendency seems to be that there are fewer active users during the weekends (days 6, 7, and 13). Additionally, it appears to be a correlation between the number of active users and the number of published activities, with day 6 and 14 being the exceptions. The spike on day 14 was likely due to the distribution of the questionnaire that day.

\textbf{Score} \\
Figure \ref{fig:engagement_points_in_category} shows the average score in each category for the participants in the two groups. The average in the student society was more than 3 three times the average of the classmates. Figure \ref{fig:engagement_duration_in_category} revealed that the average time spent hovering was approximately the same for the two groups. Thus, the noticeable difference in average score was influenced by something else than time spent hovering. One apparent reason is that cultural activities get five times the points of educational activities, hence impacting the total score. Another reason is that the student society used the friend tracking feature more than the classmates, resulting in more frequent double points (see Table \ref{tab:engagement_friend_tracking}).

\textbf{Challenges} \\
Figure \ref{fig:engagement_created_challenges} shows the seven challenges created in the testing period. We observed that only three challenges were started during the first week of testing and that these only engaged four unique players. To ensure that all participants were familiar with the in-game challenges, we created three challenges in the beginning of the second week. Two of the challenges were created for the student society and one for the classmates group. However, the challenges did not enhance the players’ engagement, with many either ignoring or declining the invites, especially in the student society group.

\textbf{Reactions} \\
The reactions given by the users are shown in Figure \ref{fig:engagement_reactions_per_group}. The student society group accounted for 89.2\% of the reactions, while the group of classmates generated 10.8\%. The student society group is larger than the group of classmates, and naturally they represent a larger number of the given reactions. However, the average number of reactions given by the two groups shows that the student society group gave reactions more frequently (see Figure \ref{fig:engagement_reactions_per_user}).

Figure \ref{fig:engagement_reactions_per_user} show the average number of reactions given by each user. The student society group gave 4.8 reactions per participant, and the classmates gave 1 reaction per participant. The blue and red linear graphs in the figure are the averages. There were six users, three from each group, that did not react to any posts at all. Additionally, there was one user (1) that used the reaction feature significantly more than any other participant, reacting to a total of 25 posts. This outlier increased the average result per user in the student society group from 3.0 to 4.8. Even if we ignore the outlier, the student society reacted to three times as many posts as the classmates.
